Fine-Tuning Is Usually the Wrong First Move

Why the expensive option feels safer, and what that costs a London team building generative features.

31 October 2026

A team sits down to plan a generative feature and almost immediately starts talking about fine-tuning. It sounds like the serious choice. It implies ownership, craft, a model shaped to your business rather than borrowed off the shelf. The trouble is that the same conversation often happens before anyone has written a good prompt, and the decision that follows is expensive, slow, and hard to reverse.

The argument here is simple and it runs against the usual instinct: in most London generative AI builds, fine-tuning is the last thing you should reach for, not the first. It is a precision tool with a narrow job, and treating it as the default buys you cost and delay in exchange for very little.

Fine-tuning changes behaviour, not knowledge

The confusion starts with what fine-tuning actually does. It retrains a model's weights on your data. That is good at teaching style, format, tone, a particular shape of output. It is poor at teaching facts, because facts change and weights do not update when your product catalogue does. Teams fine-tune hoping the model will "know" their documents, then discover it has learned the rhythm of their writing and still invents the details.

Retrieval works the other way around. It fetches the relevant material at the moment of the request and hands it to the model as context. That is faster, cheaper, and far easier to audit, because you can see which sources fed a given answer. For most factual grounding, that is the job. The choice between prompting, retrieval, and fine-tuning is the single biggest cost decision in a build, and it is the one most often made by instinct rather than evidence.

The demo hides the problem until launch

There is a second reason the expensive option wins the meeting. A fine-tune produces a satisfying artefact. You can point at it. It took weeks and a budget line, so it feels like progress. A well-constructed prompt and a retrieval layer feel like configuration, not engineering, even when they outperform the fine-tune on real inputs.

What gets skipped is the part that decides whether the feature survives: how it behaves on your actual data, at your actual volume, with your actual failure cases. A prompt library that is versioned and tested gives you that evidence cheaply and lets you change direction. A fine-tune locks you into a model state that is awkward to inspect and costly to redo. The novelty of a generate button wears off in a fortnight; what keeps users coming back is context, not training.

What a good result looks like

A healthy generative build can explain, in plain terms, why it uses the approach it uses. It starts simple, adds retrieval where grounding matters, and only reaches for fine-tuning when nothing else hits the precision the workflow needs, with the reasoning and cost comparison written down. It also settles the questions London teams now get asked early: where outputs came from, which model produced them, and whether users knew they were reading generated text.

That is the difference between a feature people return to and one that quietly comes back out of the product.
