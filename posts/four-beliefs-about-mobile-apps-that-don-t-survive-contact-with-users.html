Four Beliefs About Mobile Apps That Don't Survive Contact With Users

A look at the assumptions London teams carry into a mobile build, and why each one tends to cost them later.

30 October 2026

There's a belief that sits behind a lot of mobile projects in London: that the hard part is choosing the platform, and everything after that is execution. The platform choice matters, but it's rarely what decides whether an app feels good in someone's hand six months after launch. What decides it is a handful of assumptions that nobody says out loud — and that tend to be wrong in predictable ways.

"Cross-platform means one build, so it's the cheap route"

The appeal is obvious: one codebase, two stores, half the budget. The reality is that cross-platform is an architectural decision, not a discount. Teams that treat it as a shortcut tend to end up with the worst of both worlds — an app that doesn't quite feel native on either platform, and a maintenance story that's messier than two clean codebases would have been. The question worth asking early isn't "can we share code?" but "what are we willing to let feel slightly off, and does that matter for this product?" That's a design conversation, not a tooling one.

"If it runs on the simulator, it runs"

Simulators are convenient and they are also liars. They run on a fast machine with plenty of memory and none of the mess of a real phone — the background apps, the old battery, the patchy signal on the train. An app that behaves perfectly in a simulator can stutter or crash on a three-year-old handset, and the person holding that handset won't file a bug report. They'll just stop opening it. Testing on real hardware isn't a nice-to-have at the end of a project; it's the only honest signal you get before strangers start using the thing.

"Sluggish is a polish problem we can fix later"

Performance gets treated as something you sand down near launch. But the feel of an app — how quickly a screen responds, whether a scroll glides or drags — is a product decision that gets baked in early, often by choices about architecture and data flow that are expensive to change later. Users don't compare your app to a spec sheet. They compare it to every other app on their phone, and the bar is set by the best one they use daily. Retention numbers tend to reflect this long before anyone on the team is willing to say it out loud.

"Two platforms means two teams and two budgets"

Separate iOS and Android codebases do double the cost and halve the speed — but the subtler cost is parity. One platform quietly falls behind, features land on one store first, and the users on the other side notice. The opposite mistake is just as common: assuming a single codebase automatically solves parity, when in practice it just moves the problem into how carefully the shared code is written and tested. Either way, the decision is about where you want to spend your complexity budget, and it's worth being deliberate about it rather than defaulting.

What a good result looks like is unglamorous: an app that feels like it belongs on the phone, tested on hardware real people own, with the source and documentation in your hands so the next developer isn't starting from guesswork. Getting there is mostly about catching these assumptions before they harden into architecture.
