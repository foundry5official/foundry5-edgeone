Notebook, Platform, or Partner: Three Routes to a Working Model

A plain comparison of the three ways London teams usually try to get machine learning into production, and who each one actually suits.

28 October 2026

Most teams reach the same fork without naming it. A model shows promise on a sample, someone asks what happens next, and the room splits into three camps. One wants to keep it in-house, one wants to buy a platform and let it handle the rest, and one wants to bring in a partner. Each route is defensible. Each fails for different reasons, and for different kinds of team.

The in-house build

This is the route where your own engineers own the notebook and everything downstream of it. It suits organisations that already have data engineering capacity, a clear owner for the model after launch, and enough volume of work to justify building the surrounding machinery rather than renting it. The appeal is control and long-term cost.

Where it goes wrong is scope. The modelling is often the smallest part of the job. The pipeline, the serving layer, the monitoring for drift, and the documentation that lets someone else retrain the thing are all real work, and they are frequently left out of the plan. A team that has never shipped a model into production tends to underestimate that gap, and the notebook sits in a repository while the business waits.

The platform route

Buying a managed platform moves a lot of the undifferentiated plumbing off your plate. It suits teams with a modest, well-understood use case and limited appetite to run infrastructure — a recommendation feature, a scoring model, something close to a pattern the platform already supports well.

The trade-off is fit. Platforms are built around common shapes of problem, and your data rarely arrives in that shape. The harder question is feasibility: whether your data can support the model at all. No platform answers that for you, and teams sometimes discover mid-build that labels are missing or that the target was never agreed. That is a data problem wearing a tooling costume.

The partner-led route

Bringing in an outside team suits organisations with data and a decision it should be informing, but without the internal bench to build and then own the system. It also fits teams holding a working notebook that needs to become something operational.

The risk here is dependency without handover. A partner who leaves you with a black box and no documentation has solved the short-term problem and created a longer one. The good version of this route ends with your team able to run and retrain the model themselves, and with the training data staying where your compliance people expect it. If the arrangement does not include that, it is not really finished.

What a good outcome looks like

Whichever route you pick, the shape of success is the same. Someone checked in week one whether the data could carry the model, rather than in month three after the budget was spent. The thing that ships is a system with a pipeline and monitoring, not a notebook with a good accuracy figure. And there is a named owner, plus documentation, so performance decay gets noticed by a dashboard rather than by a customer.

None of that is exotic. It is just usually skipped. The choice between the three routes matters far less than whether the boring parts are in scope from the start, and that is the question worth asking before anyone opens a notebook.
