Four Mistakes That Undermine LLM Integration in London

The recurring errors that turn a promising model demo into a production problem, and why they matter more than the model itself.

29 October 2026

Most teams treat an LLM integration as a wiring job: get a key, point a prompt at an endpoint, render whatever comes back. It looks finished within a week. That is usually the point where the trouble starts, because the parts that decide whether the feature survives contact with real users sit outside the model call. What follows are the mistakes that show up again and again in London teams building on Claude, GPT and open models — not the exotic ones, the ordinary ones.

Choosing the model by reputation rather than by task

The first decision most teams make is which model to use, and it is often made on the strength of a launch post or a colleague's recommendation. The consequence is quiet but expensive: a premium model handling work a cheaper one would do just as well, or a fast, small model placed where its accuracy falls short. Latency is the same story from the other direction. A model that responds beautifully in a demo can be too slow for a feature where someone is waiting on the answer.

Model choice is an engineering judgement about accuracy, cost, speed and data handling, and it has to be made per task rather than once for the whole product. Teams that skip the comparison rarely notice the cost until the invoice arrives, and by then the architecture has settled around the wrong choice.

Treating grounding as optional

An ungrounded model answers from whatever it absorbed during training, which means it will produce a confident, well-written response that has no basis in your product's actual content. In a demo this reads as impressive. In front of a paying customer it reads as a company that does not know its own policies.

Grounding the model in your own data changes the nature of the feature: answers come from material you control and can be traced back to a source. Teams that defer this work often assume it is a later refinement. It is closer to a foundation, and retrofitting it after launch means revisiting prompts, data pipelines and test coverage at once.

Shipping without a way to tell if it still works

A prompt tweak or a provider's model update can change behaviour without anyone touching the surrounding code. Without a test suite for model outputs, that change surfaces as a support ticket weeks later, and nobody can say which alteration caused it. This is the mistake that costs the most engineering time, because it turns every change into a gamble.

Regression testing for LLM features is unfamiliar territory for teams used to deterministic software, which is precisely why it gets skipped. The result is a product that works until it quietly doesn't.

Leaving cost and failure unmanaged

Inference costs do not arrive as a steady line. They compound with usage, and a busy week or a looping prompt can move the bill sharply. Equally, providers have bad days: a slow endpoint or an outage will take a feature down unless something graceful happens when the call fails. Neither concern appears in a demo, and both become incidents in production.

A well-built integration treats token budgets, caching and fallbacks as part of the feature rather than an optimisation for later. Teams that don't often find themselves explaining an outage or an unexpected bill to people who assumed the AI was handled.

What good looks like

A sound integration is one where you can say, with evidence, why each model was chosen, where its answers come from, what happens when a provider stumbles, and what a change will cost. That is a different kind of work from wiring an API key, and it is what separates a feature that lasts from one that gets quietly switched off.
